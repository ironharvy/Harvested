# Chapter 10: The Night Shift

Maren left her flat at twenty past ten.

The streets of the eastern district were empty. The drizzle had stopped, but the cobblestones were still wet, catching the orange glow of the street lamps in long, broken reflections. The air smelled of damp brick and ozone — the mineral tang of the grid, sharper at night when the city was quiet enough to notice it. Maren walked in her Bureau coveralls and her work boots, her lanyard and key in her pocket, her hands at her sides. She did not hurry. A technician walking to the station at half past ten was not unusual — shift overlaps, emergency callouts, forgotten paperwork. She had a reason prepared if anyone asked. Nobody asked.

The perimeter fence came into view. The station was lit — it was always lit, the grid never sleeping — but the exterior lights were dimmer at night, reduced to the security lamps at the gate and the blue-white glow leaking from the ventilation stacks. The conduit spire stood against the sky like a dark finger, its copper tip invisible in the overcast. Fenne's security hut was closed; the night guard was someone Maren did not know by name, a shape behind glass who glanced at her badge and waved her through.

She badged through the main entrance. The lobby was dim, the strip lights on their overnight setting. The intake bay door was shut. The corridor to the stairwell was empty. She could hear, from somewhere above, the faint sound of a radio — Gren's, probably, in the monitoring centre, tuned to the late-night talk station that kept her company through the small hours.

Maren checked her watch. Ten thirty-two. Gren's first round would begin at ten thirty, give or take a few minutes. B2 first — that was the pattern. Down the stairwell to B2, a walk through both quadrants, check the readouts on the surge cells, log any anomalies. Twenty minutes. Then back up to B1 for the same. Then the ground floor. Then back to the monitoring centre for a coffee and the next cycle.

If Gren was on B2 now, the B1 floor was empty.

Maren descended.

The stairwell was the same stairwell she had walked down a thousand times — concrete walls, caged bulbs, the temperature dropping with each landing. The air grew drier, more charged. She passed the B1 door and kept going, stopping at the half-landing between B1 and B2, where a narrow window looked onto the B2 stairwell door. She waited. Listened.

The hum was louder here. Not muted by two floors of concrete, but close — the combined psychic output of the station's containment floors pressing against the walls, the floor, the air. It was a sound you felt more than heard: a vibration in the sternum, in the sinuses, in the small bones of the inner ear. Maren had lived inside this sound for twelve years. Tonight it was not background. It was the sound of hundreds of people, held in glass, being consumed.

A door opened below her. The heavy thud of the B2 security door, then footsteps — Gren's, unmistakable, the measured tread of a woman who walked the same route four nights a week and did not vary her pace. The footsteps moved away, into B2's main corridor. Gren was on her round.

Maren went back up to B1.

The security door required her badge and her four-digit code. She entered both. The lock released with a click that sounded, in the empty stairwell, like a bone breaking. She opened the door and stepped through into the containment hall.

B1 was as she had left it — as it always was, as it would be tomorrow and the day after and for decades to come, until the cells were empty and the station was decommissioned and the concrete was left to weather into Ashward's grey landscape. The fluorescent strips cast their flat, shadowless light. The racks rose on both sides of the main corridor — steel frames holding glass-and-copper cylinders, row upon row, the suppression sigils catching the light in fine lines that looked almost beautiful if you did not know what they were doing.

The hum was the source here. Not transmitted, not muted — the thing itself. Several hundred contained souls producing a steady psychic current that Maren felt in her teeth, in her fingertips, in the place behind her eyes where headaches begin. She had worked in this sound for twelve years. She had calibrated cells while it pressed against her skull. She had never, until this moment, heard it as voices.

She turned into the eastern quadrant. The aisle was narrow — one person wide, no room for a calibration cart tonight, no room for anything but Maren and the cells and the dim awareness that she was walking past rows of contained people on her way to free one of them.

B1-4427. Second row, third from the left, shoulder height. The readout panel glowed green in the flat light. Output nominal. Suppression 93%. Yield-to-depletion: 7.7 years. The number had dropped again. Two tenths of a year lost in two days. The erosion was not slowing. It was not going to slow.

Maren stood before the cell. The glass had the same faint luminescence she had seen on her first visit — that cold blue-white thinning at the interior surface, like light held just below the point of visibility. At 93% suppression, it was fractionally brighter. Not enough to notice unless you were looking. She was looking.

She reached for her lanyard. The technician's key was warm from her pocket — a small, unremarkable key, standard-issue, one of thirty in the station. It fit the access panel on every containment cell on every floor. She had used it a thousand times for maintenance. She had never used it for this.

She looked down the aisle. Empty. The hum was steady, unbroken, unchanging. Gren was on B2. The stairwell was quiet. The monitoring centre was two floors above, and the radio was playing, and no one was watching the feed from B1 because no one watched the feed from B1 at night — it was Gren's job to walk the floor, not to watch the screen, and the screen showed what it always showed: green, green, green, green.

Maren inserted the key into the access panel of B1-4427.

The panel opened. Inside: the manual release lever, the conduit coupling, and the base of the sigil array — four micro-dials and the etched copper plate that held the suppression pattern. The lever was steel, painted red at the handle in the universal language of do-not-touch. It had not been touched since the cell was installed.

She did not pull it. Not yet. The sigils had to come off first.

Maren reached for the micro-dials. She turned each one to zero. The suppression field collapsed — not with a sound, not with a visible effect, but with a change in the quality of the air around the cell, a sudden charged warmth, like the moment before lightning. The readout flickered. The green indicator shifted to amber, then back to green, then to amber again, cycling as the system tried to reconcile a suppression reading of zero with a cell that was still physically intact.

The luminescence behind the glass brightened.

Maren's hands were steady. Her breathing was even. Her heart beat in her chest like something caged, but her hands — her hands were twelve years trained, and they did what they had always done.

She gripped the lever.
