# Chapter 6: What Jorin Could Not Give

She told him on Day Five, in the break room, over sandwiches.

It was not planned. Maren had not rehearsed what to say or decided that today was the day. She had come into the break room at noon the way she always did, taken her sandwich from the refrigerator, sat across from Jorin at the Formica table with its six mismatched chairs, and listened to him talk about the tram delays and his daughter's school play and the calibration drift on B2 that had finally resolved itself overnight. The safety poster on the wall curled at its corners. The coffee machine gurgled. The window, set too high in the wall to see out of, let in a trapezoid of grey light that moved across the table as the minutes passed.

And then she said it, because the alternative was carrying it alone for another hour, another day, another shift of watching the numbers and saying nothing.

"My mother's in B1."

Jorin stopped chewing. He looked at her — not with surprise, exactly, but with the careful stillness of a man calculating what he had just heard against what he wished he hadn't.

"Your mother," he said.

"B1-4427. Eastern quadrant. High-draw cell. No deferral."

Jorin set his sandwich down. He was quiet for a moment. The coffee machine finished its cycle and clicked off. Somewhere below them, through the floor, the hum continued.

"When did she—"

"Several weeks ago. I found the intake record on Monday."

"Maren." He said her name the way you say someone's name when you don't know what else to say — as a placeholder, a breath between understanding and not wanting to.

"The deferral field was zero. The petition was filed. Someone — Poul, or someone above Poul — didn't process it. She went straight in."

Jorin rubbed his beard with one hand. The gesture was habitual, a thing he did when he was thinking, and Maren had seen it a hundred times over years of shared shifts and break-room lunches and the small, sustaining rituals of workplace companionship. She had never before noticed how much it looked like a man steeling himself.

"Have you talked to Leth?"

"No."

"Are you going to?"

"What would I say? My mother's soul is in a cell I walk past every day, and I'd like it back?"

Jorin did not smile. "You could file a formal inquiry. The deferral should have held — if the petition was filed and it wasn't processed, that's a procedural breach. You have grounds."

"A formal inquiry goes through the Regional Directorate. It would take months. The yield-to-depletion on her cell is 8.1 years and dropping. She's in a high-draw cell, Jorin. She's degrading faster than she should be."

He heard what she wasn't saying. She watched him hear it — the slight tightening around his eyes, the way his hand stopped moving on his beard and went flat against the table.

"What are you thinking about doing?"

Maren looked at the trapezoid of light on the table. It had moved while they were talking. "I'm thinking about the manual override."

The silence that followed had a particular weight. It was not the silence of shock — Jorin was not a naive man, and he had worked in the station long enough to know what the override was and what it did. It was the silence of a man who understood what he was being told and was measuring the distance between sympathy and risk.

"Maren," he said again, and this time her name was not a placeholder. It was a warning.

"I know."

"If they catch you — and they would catch you, there are logs, there are sensor records, Gren does rounds—"

"I know the rounds. I know the logs."

"Then you know that what you're describing is a criminal act. Destruction of a civic resource. The Act is clear on that. You'd lose your certification, your pension, your—"

"I know what I'd lose."

Jorin looked at her across the table. His face held something that Maren recognised because she had worn it herself for twelve years: the expression of a person who understood that the system was wrong and who had decided, long ago and without drama, that understanding was as far as they would go. It was not cowardice. It was the arithmetic of ordinary life — a wife, a daughter, a mortgage, a pension, the accumulated weight of everything you had built inside the system's walls and could not afford to set on fire.

"I have a daughter," Jorin said. He did not say it as an excuse. He said it as a fact, the way the readout panels displayed yield-to-depletion as a fact — a number that determined what was possible and what was not.

"I know," Maren said. "I'm not asking you to help."

"Then what are you asking?"

She thought about it. What was she asking? Not for his hands or his key or his presence in B1 on the night she would go down. Not for him to be an accomplice, a lookout, a co-conspirator. She was asking for something smaller and, in its way, harder: for him to know, and to say nothing.

"I'm asking you not to report it."

Jorin exhaled. He picked up his sandwich, looked at it, and put it down again. The coffee machine stood silent on the counter. The high window let in its grey light.

"I won't report you," he said. "I won't help you either."

"I know."

"If Leth asks me if something's wrong, I'll say I don't know anything. That's true enough."

"It is."

"But Maren—" He leaned forward. The laugh lines around his eyes were deep, and they were not from laughing. "Even if you do this. Even if you get the cell open and the soul disperses and nobody catches you on the night. They'll know. There'll be an empty cell and a missing soul and a gap in the logs. It will come back to you."

"Probably."

"And then what?"

Maren did not answer, because the answer was that she did not know, and that not knowing was, for the first time in twelve years, not the same as not caring. She finished her sandwich. Jorin finished his. They disposed of the wrappers and rinsed their mugs and went back to the monitoring centre, and the afternoon passed the way afternoons passed, and neither of them mentioned it again.

But something had shifted between them — not a rift, nothing so dramatic. A redistribution of weight. Jorin had given her what he could: his silence, his refusal to betray her, the bounded gift of looking away. He had not given her what she needed, which was another pair of hands or another voice that said *yes, do it, the system is wrong and this is right*. He could not give that. He had a daughter, and the daughter was real, and the arithmetic was clear.

Maren did not blame him. She had been Jorin for twelve years. She knew the math.

She went home that evening and took the folded notes from the pocket of her coveralls and smoothed them on the kitchen table. Suppression target. Access sequence. Timing window. The plan was there, sketched in the shorthand of her trade, and now she knew she would execute it alone, and the knowledge was cold and steady and did not waver.
