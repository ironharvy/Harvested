# Chapter 7: The Closing Door

The summons came on Day Seven, in the form of a handwritten note slipped under the monitoring centre door before Maren's shift began: *When you have a moment — CL.*

CL. Cassan Leth. The Director's initials, written in the measured hand of a man who did not use email for things he considered personal. Maren folded the note and put it in her pocket and felt something tighten in the muscles of her shoulders that she chose to interpret as nothing.

She went at ten, after the morning scan. Leth's office was on the first floor — a room with a window overlooking the station's internal courtyard and the base of the conduit spire, a metal desk that had not been replaced since the last refurbishment, and filing cabinets that gave the air the smell of old paper and dust and weak tea. Leth was behind the desk when she knocked, his charcoal suit buttoned, his silver Bureau pin at the lapel, his broad face arranged in the expression of patient concern that was his default and that, today, felt less default than usual.

"Maren. Sit down."

She sat.

"How are you?" he asked, and the question was not perfunctory. Leth was not a man who asked how-are-you as a corridor greeting. When he asked, he meant it, and the sincerity made Maren's chest constrict in a way she had not expected.

"I'm fine, Director."

"You've seemed a bit off this week. Nothing I can put a finger on — just a sense." He folded his hands on the desk. The hands were soft, uncalloused. He had not touched a containment cell in twenty years. "I know you lost your mother recently. I wanted to check in."

"I appreciate that."

"Grief has its own schedule, Maren. The Bureau allows bereavement leave — you took a day, as I recall. If you need more, it's available."

"I'm managing."

Leth nodded. He studied her with the benign attentiveness of a man who had managed people for decades and who trusted his own instincts about them — a trust that was, in this case, not misplaced. He could see that Maren was not fine. He interpreted this as grief, which was partly true, and not as planning, which was the rest of it.

"I ask because your work has been exemplary, as always. I don't want to lose that to something that a few days' rest could prevent."

"I'm managing, Director. Truly."

He held her gaze a moment longer, then let it go with a small nod. "My door is open. You know that."

"I do."

She left his office and walked back to the monitoring centre along the first-floor corridor, past the records room and the small staff kitchen where someone had left a kettle boiling. The corridor smelled of steam and the particular staleness of recirculated air. The note in her pocket felt heavier than paper should.

Leth's concern was genuine. That was the thing Maren could not set aside. He was not probing her for information or laying a trap. He was being a good manager — attending to a subordinate he valued, offering what the institution permitted him to offer: bereavement leave, an open door, the quiet acknowledgment that people who maintain containment cells full of captured souls sometimes need a day off. His care was real, and it was the care of a man who believed profoundly in the system they both served, and the two things — the care and the belief — were not in contradiction because to Leth there was nothing contradictory about them.

She returned to her console. The screens showed their data. Green, green, amber, green. The shift continued.

At two o'clock, a compliance notification appeared in Maren's queue.

It was a routine audit flag — the kind generated automatically when the Bureau's internal monitoring systems detected an unusual pattern in a technician's record-access logs. Maren had accessed the B1 eastern quadrant intake records three times in the past week, the containment cell maintenance manual once, and the station cell map twice. Individually, each access was within her normal duties. Collectively, they formed a pattern that the system's compliance algorithm had flagged for review.

The flag was not personal. It was not directed at Maren specifically. The algorithm ran across all station-level personnel on a rolling cycle, and anyone whose access pattern deviated from their baseline would trigger the same notification. The flag went to the compliance officer — in Station Nine's case, that was Director Leth himself, because the station was understaffed and the compliance role was one of several that had been folded into the Director's portfolio years ago.

Maren stared at the notification. It told her, in the flat bureaucratic language of compliance systems, that her recent record accesses had been noted and that a review period of fourteen days would follow, during which she should ensure her access patterns were consistent with her assigned duties. There was no accusation. There was no follow-up interview. There was only the system, grinding through its own machinery, catching her in a net that had not been cast for her.

She closed the notification and filed it in her queue. Her hands were steady. Her face was composed. Inside, the window was narrowing.

She thought about Gren's schedule. The night-shift supervisor ran her rounds on a pattern Maren had mapped during her planning: B2 first, then B1, then the ground floor. The cycle took forty minutes. But Gren's schedule rotated — four nights on, two off — and the nights she was off were covered by a junior supervisor whose rounds were less predictable. Maren needed Gren's shift. She needed the pattern she had mapped. The compliance flag meant she could not afford to wait weeks; the longer she delayed, the more access anomalies would accrue, and the fourteen-day review period would produce a summary that Leth would read.

She checked Gren's schedule on the staff rota, pinned to the noticeboard in the monitoring centre. Gren's next run of four nights began in five days. After that, a two-day break. After that, another four-night run. But B1-4427's yield-to-depletion had dropped to 7.9 years on the morning's readout — down from 8.1 two days ago. The erosion was accelerating. Every day Maren waited was a day of degradation she could not reverse.

Five days. Gren would be on shift, running her predictable rounds. The compliance review would be in its first week — noted but not yet summarised. Maren's access to B1 during a day shift was unremarkable; she went down to recalibrate cells regularly. She could lower B1-4427's suppression by one or two points — from 99% to 94%, say — during a routine visit. The console would still show green. No flag. No work order. And then, on the fifth night, when Gren was on her B2 round and the B1 floor was empty, Maren could go down.

One evening. One window. One chance.

Maren saved the compliance notification to her personal archive and deleted it from her active queue. She ran the afternoon scan. She filed her reports. She drank the last cup of coffee from the machine and washed the mug and set it on the draining board. The monitoring centre hummed. The light through the narrow window was grey and fading.

When she left the station that evening, she passed Leth's office. The door was closed. A strip of warm light showed beneath it — he was still inside, working late, being the steady, sincere, believing man he had always been. The smell of weak tea drifted through the gap.

Maren walked past. Five days. The door was closing, and she would have to move through it before it shut.
