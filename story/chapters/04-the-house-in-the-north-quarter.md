# Chapter 4: The House in the North Quarter

The tram north took twenty minutes and passed through three versions of Ashward: the industrial fringe around Station Nine, where the buildings were concrete and the streets were wide and empty; the city centre, where the shops were open and the pavements held a thin crowd of people going about the business of a Tuesday evening; and the north quarter, where the terraces were older, the cobbles showed through cracked tarmac, and the tram stopped at a corner Maren had not stood on in four months.

She walked three streets east. The terraced row was unchanged — brick facades darkened by decades of soot, iron railings, ground-floor windows with nets or blinds. Number fourteen had blinds, cream-coloured, half-drawn the way Irina had always kept them. Maren stood on the step and used her key. The lock turned with the same reluctant click it had made since she was a child.

The hallway smelled of dust and, beneath the dust, the ghost of lavender soap. Irina had used the same soap for as long as Maren could remember — a brand sold at the chemist's two streets over, in a purple paper wrapper. The smell was not strong. It was the memory of a smell, the way the warmth in a recently vacated room is the memory of a body. Maren closed the door behind her and stood in the hallway and breathed it in.

The flat was small. Two bedrooms — one that had been Maren's until she was twenty-two, now used for storage; one that was Irina's, the door half-open, the bed made. A sitting room where the curtains were half-drawn and the television sat dark on its stand. A kitchen.

Maren went to the kitchen.

The table had two chairs, though only one had been used. Maren could tell which one: the seat cushion on the left was compressed, shaped to Irina's slight weight, while the one on the right — Maren's chair, once — was plump and undented. On the table, a folded newspaper from the week Irina died, still open to the crossword. Three clues filled in, in Irina's small, careful handwriting. The rest blank. Maren looked at the answers. Seven across: *routine*. Twelve down: *patience*. Fifteen across: *enough*.

She sat in the right-hand chair. The cushion gave beneath her with a softness that felt like accusation.

The kitchen was as Irina had left it. The kettle on the counter, angled just so — handle pointing toward the window, because Irina was left-handed and had arranged everything for her own reach. A mug beside it, washed and inverted on the draining board. The dish towel folded over the oven handle. The damp patch on the wall above the sink, spreading in a slow brown bloom that Irina had mentioned every time Maren visited and that neither of them had ever done anything about. It was larger now. It had kept growing after Irina stopped.

On the windowsill above the sink, a row of objects that Irina had collected without apparent system: a smooth stone from a beach trip Maren barely remembered, a tin of buttons, a small glass jar that had once held jam and now held pencils. Beside the pencils, a Bureau of Reclamation leaflet — the standard one, *Civic Energy and You*, distributed to households every few years. Maren picked it up. The paper was soft with handling. Irina had read it, or at least held it, and the thought of her mother sitting at this table reading the Bureau's cheerful explanation of why the dead owed the living made something in Maren's chest tighten like a fist.

She put the leaflet down.

Irina's shoes were by the front door. Brown lace-ups, sensible, slightly worn at the heel. Her coat hung on the hook — a navy wool thing, too warm for the season she died in, waiting for an autumn that would come without her. In the bathroom, the lavender soap sat in its dish beside the tap, a sliver now, used nearly through. The towel on the rail was dry and stiff.

Maren moved through the rooms the way you move through a museum after hours — carefully, aware that the objects are not yours, that they belong to a context you have been admitted to but do not inhabit. These were her mother's things. They held the shape of her mother's days: the kettle angled for a left hand, the chair worn to a specific weight, the crossword half-finished because there had not been time to finish it, or because the answers had stopped mattering, or because Irina had simply set the pen down and not picked it up again.

She returned to the kitchen and sat. The window above the sink showed the narrow back yard — a square of concrete with a drain, a clothesline with two pegs still clipped to it, and the brick wall of the next terrace. The light was going. The sky above the wall was the same grey it had been all day, darkening now to something closer to graphite.

Maren thought about the deferral petition. Someone had filed it — she was almost certain. A neighbour, perhaps the same one who had called Maren with the news. The petition should have held. Sixty days. Time for the kind of grief that institutions permit: orderly, bounded, concluded by a date on a form. Instead, the field read zero, and Irina's soul was already in B1-4427, already degrading at a rate the actuarial tables would call efficient.

She thought about the luminescence behind the glass. That faint, cold blue-white pressing against the inside of the cylinder, like a hand against a frosted window. Her mother. Her mother, who had kept the kettle angled for her left hand and the blinds half-drawn and the damp patch growing and the crossword three clues from wherever she had meant to stop.

Maren did not make a decision in any dramatic sense. There was no moment of resolve, no internal declaration, no clenched jaw or hardened eye. She sat in her mother's kitchen in the thickening dusk, in the chair that had not been sat in for years, and she understood — not decided, understood — that she would not let the grid have this. She did not yet know what that meant. She did not have a plan. She had only the certainty, as plain and unadorned as the kettle on the counter, that the luminescence in B1-4427 was her mother, and that the system that was consuming her had no right to it, and that Maren had spent twelve years helping it consume others and had never once felt this, and that the feeling, now that it was here, was not going away.

She locked the door behind her when she left. The tram back to the eastern district was nearly empty. The brass plaque near the door caught the overhead light, and this time Maren read it, and the words were the same words they had always been, and they meant something they had not meant yesterday.

She went home. She did not sleep well.
