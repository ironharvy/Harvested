## Seed (from README)

# Harvested
A story of infrastructure powered by the souls of deceased

## Interrogative questions

### 1. What kind of infrastructure runs on the souls of the dead?

**Proposed answer:** A city's entire civic infrastructure — electricity, heating, water purification, transit — is powered by soul-furnaces: massive engines housed beneath the city that burn the spiritual residue of the recently deceased. The technology was discovered a century ago when an engineer noticed that the moment of death released a measurable energy signature. The city has since industrialised the process. Every citizen's death contract guarantees their soul to the municipal grid. The lights never go out.

**Accepted answer:** Same as proposed.

### 2. Who is the protagonist, and what draws them into the story's central conflict?

**Proposed answer:** Lena Morrow, a mid-level furnace technician — a "stoker" — who maintains the soul-furnaces in Sector 7, one of the city's older and less efficient districts. She is competent, pragmatic, and largely unbothered by the ethics of her work (she grew up with the system; everyone did). The conflict begins when she recognises a specific soul-signature in the furnace feed: her recently deceased mother, who was supposed to have been granted a "quiet release" — an exemption from harvesting that Lena paid her entire savings to secure. Someone diverted her mother's soul back into the grid. Lena's investigation pulls her from the mechanical layer into the political one.

**Accepted answer:** Same as proposed.

### 3. What are the stakes — what happens if the protagonist fails or succeeds?

**Proposed answer:** If Lena fails, her mother's soul is consumed entirely — burned to nothing, no afterlife, no rest, just fuel. But the deeper stakes are systemic: Lena discovers that "quiet release" exemptions are a fraud. Every soul is harvested regardless; the exemption programme is a revenue scheme that exploits grief. If she exposes this, the city's social contract collapses — citizens accepted soul-harvesting because they believed in the opt-out. Without that safety valve, riots, sabotage, and infrastructure collapse become real possibilities. If she succeeds in freeing her mother but stays silent about the fraud, she saves one soul and condemns the system to continue. The story forces her to choose between the personal and the political.

**Accepted answer:** Same as proposed.

### 4. What is the tone, and what genre territory does this occupy?

**Proposed answer:** Secondary-world industrial fantasy with noir undertones — think gas-lit furnace rooms, bureaucratic corruption, and a protagonist who talks to the dead through instrument readings. The tone is sombre but not bleak; there's dark humour in the mundanity of soul-processing (maintenance logs, union grievances, shift rotations alongside the metaphysical horror of what the work actually is). The story earns its emotional weight through specificity — the strangeness of the world is grounded in workplace detail. It is not horror; it is a story about labour, grief, and complicity told through a fantastical lens.

**Accepted answer:** Same as proposed.

### 5. What is the target length, and does the scope suit a short story or something longer?

**Proposed answer:** A novelette of roughly 12,000–18,000 words. The concept has enough structural complexity (personal mystery → systemic revelation → moral choice) to need room, but the cast is small and the timeline is compressed (days, not months). A novelette gives space for atmosphere and workplace texture without requiring subplot sprawl. Three acts, nine to twelve chapters.

**Accepted answer:** Same as proposed.

## Foundation for the Premise phase

The story is set in Ashmark, a sprawling industrial city built above a network of underground soul-furnaces. The technology — called thanalic engineering — converts the energy released at the moment of death (and the residual spiritual signature that persists afterward) into usable power. A century of refinement has made the process efficient and invisible to most citizens; the furnaces hum beneath their feet, and the lights stay on.

Lena Morrow is a stoker — a furnace technician in Sector 7. Her work is unglamorous: calibrating burn rates, clearing residue blockages, filing incident reports. She does not think of herself as someone who burns souls; she thinks of herself as someone who keeps the heat on. This changes when she detects her mother's soul-signature in a feed that should never have contained it.

The investigation takes her through layers of the city's infrastructure: the Registrar of Departures (which processes death contracts), the Quiet Release Bureau (which supposedly handles exemptions), and eventually the office of the Chief Municipal Engineer, who oversees the entire thanalic grid. Along the way she is helped — reluctantly — by Edric Calloway, a Registrar clerk who has been falsifying records under duress, and opposed by Superintendent Voss, who runs Sector 7 and has a personal stake in the exemption fraud continuing.

The thematic core is complicity: what does it mean to participate in a system you benefit from but that is built on exploitation? Lena is not an outsider or a rebel; she is a worker inside the machine. Her arc is not about discovering that the system is evil (she always knew, on some level) but about being forced to act once the abstraction becomes personal.
