## Seed (from README)

# Harvested
A story of infrastructure powered by the souls of deceased

## Interrogative questions

### 1. What kind of infrastructure runs on the souls of the dead, and how does it work?

**Proposed answer:** The story is set in a world where the electrical grid — power plants, substations, the hum beneath every city — is fuelled not by coal or uranium but by captured human souls. At death, a bureaucratic apparatus called the Bureau of Reclamation siphons the soul from the body and binds it into a containment cell. Thousands of cells stack into racks inside vast "harvest stations." Each soul generates a slow, steady output of psychic energy that turbines convert to electricity. The process is not instantaneous destruction; a soul erodes over decades, its personality fraying into static, and eventually it dissipates — fully consumed. The technology was reverse-engineered from a natural phenomenon: ghost lights that flickered near mass graves during the Consolidation Wars, two centuries ago.

**Accepted answer:** Same as proposed.

### 2. Who is the protagonist, and what is their personal stake?

**Proposed answer:** Maren Voss is a mid-level "conduit technician" — she maintains the containment cells and monitors soul degradation at Harvest Station Nine, a crumbling facility on the edge of a rust-belt city called Ashward. Maren is competent, quiet, and morally numb; she's done this work for twelve years and has learned not to think about what the flickering readouts mean. Her stake becomes personal when she discovers that her recently deceased mother's soul was fast-tracked into Station Nine's grid — not following the standard sixty-day grieving deferral — and is already showing signs of rapid erosion. Maren must decide whether to risk everything to extract her mother's soul before it's consumed, or accept that the system she serves has a right to it.

**Accepted answer:** Same as proposed.

### 3. What is the central conflict, and who or what opposes the protagonist?

**Proposed answer:** The central conflict is Maren against the institution she works for. The Bureau of Reclamation is not cartoonishly evil — it provides real power to real people, and most citizens accept the soul-harvest as a civic duty, the way we accept organ donation or taxation. Maren's direct antagonist is her supervisor, Director Cassan Leth, a true believer who sees the harvest as a moral good: the dead serve the living, and sentiment about individual souls is selfish nostalgia. The deeper conflict is internal: Maren has been complicit for years, and saving her mother means confronting the fact that every soul she ever processed was someone's mother, someone's child. The system's opposition is bureaucratic, legal, and deeply normalised — not a villain in a tower, but a stamped form and a locked door.

**Accepted answer:** Same as proposed.

### 4. What is the thematic core — what is this story really about?

**Proposed answer:** Harvested is about complicity — the quiet violence of participating in a system you know is wrong because it keeps the lights on. It asks: at what point does personal loss crack open the numbness that lets ordinary people serve monstrous machinery? It is also about the commodification of human life and what happens when we reduce people to resources. The soul-as-fuel metaphor maps onto labour exploitation, organ trafficking, fossil fuel extraction from land sacred to displaced peoples — any system where human cost is externalised and euphemised. The story does not offer an easy answer; extracting one soul doesn't dismantle the grid.

**Accepted answer:** Same as proposed.

### 5. What is the scope and structure — short story or novel, and how does it end?

**Proposed answer:** A short story of roughly 8,000–12,000 words (novelette length). Three acts: Act 1 establishes Maren's routine, the world's normalised horror, and the discovery about her mother. Act 2 follows her covert attempt to extract the soul — navigating bureaucratic obstacles, enlisting a reluctant ally, and confronting the moral weight of choosing one soul over thousands. Act 3 is the extraction attempt itself, its consequences, and the ambiguous aftermath. The ending is bittersweet: Maren succeeds in freeing her mother's soul, but the soul is already damaged — her mother's ghost recognises her only for a moment before dissolving into light. The grid keeps humming. Maren walks out of the station into the dawn, changed but alone, the infrastructure of the city glowing behind her. She does not go back to work.

## Foundation for the Premise phase

**Setting:** The city of Ashward — an industrial, grey, rust-belt metropolis whose skyline is dominated by the cooling towers and conduit spires of Harvest Station Nine. The era is vaguely contemporary but divergent: the technology is recognisably modern (electric trains, fluorescent offices, digital readouts) but the energy source is occult. The atmosphere is overcast, bureaucratic, and quietly oppressive.

**Tone:** Literary speculative fiction. Grounded, melancholy, and restrained — the horror is in the mundanity, not in gore or shock. The world's monstrousness is presented as normal because to its inhabitants it is.

**Core tension:** Individual conscience vs. systemic complicity. One person's grief cracking the shell of institutional numbness.

**Stakes:** Maren's mother's soul (personal), Maren's sense of self and complicity (internal), the unexamined moral foundation of the entire energy system (thematic).
