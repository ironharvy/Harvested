## Segment 1 of 3 — "The Morning Commute"

**Style of Music:**
```
Dark ambient spoken word, documentary underscore, sparse industrial drone, minimal piano, 
atmospheric field recordings, female narrator, contemplative pace, melancholy, cinematic, 60 BPM
```

**Lyrics:**
```
[Narration] [Slow, measured]

The tram rattled east through Ashward's grey morning,
its windows fogged at the edges
with the breath of a dozen passengers who did not look at one another.

[Pause]

Maren Voss sat near the back
with her hands in the pockets of her Bureau-issued coveralls,
watching the city slide past
in a wash of wet cobblestones and shuttered shopfronts.

The overhead cables sang in a frequency just below hearing.

[Pause]

Near the door, bolted to the wall at knee height,
a small brass plaque read:
This service is powered by the Ashward Municipal Grid —
a civic partnership with the Bureau of Reclamation.
She had ridden this tram four thousand times
and could not have told you whether the plaque was brass or bronze.

[Pause] [Darker tone]

The eastern district came into view through the drizzle:
pebble-dash terraces with curtains still drawn,
a disused rail siding rusting behind a chain-link fence,
and then, occupying a full city block,
the flat concrete rectangle of Harvest Station Nine.

Its ventilation stacks exhaled faint columns of vapour into the overcast.
The copper-clad conduit spire rose above the roofline
like a finger held up against the sky,
green with patina except at the tip
where the transduction coils kept the metal bright.

The Bureau signage on the perimeter fence —
Authorised Personnel Only —
had faded to the colour of old teeth.

[Pause]

Maren stepped off the tram
and walked the last hundred metres through the gate,
past the security hut where old Fenne raised a hand
without looking up from his thermos,
and through the main entrance.

The lobby smelled of floor polish and ozone.

She badged through the turnstile,
climbed the stairs to the second floor,
and settled into her station in the monitoring centre
with the economy of motion
that twelve years of the same routine
had polished to something almost mechanical.

[Pause] [Fade]
```

## Segment 2 of 3 — "The Names She Did Not Read"

**Style of Music:**
```
Dark ambient spoken word, documentary underscore, low industrial hum, sparse droning cello, 
minimal electronics, female narrator, measured unsettling pace, quiet dread, cinematic, 60 BPM
```

**Lyrics:**
```
[Narration] [Steady, clinical]

The screens blinked on.
Rows of data resolved:
cell numbers, output levels, suppression integrity,
yield-to-depletion estimates.
Green indicators, mostly.
A scattering of amber.

The amber ones she would check after her first coffee.

[Pause]

The coffee came from a machine in the corner
that produced a liquid the colour of canal water,
and Maren drank it without tasting it,
the same way she read the intake logs without reading the names.

[Pause] [Quieter, weighted]

She did not read the names.

This was not a rule.
There was no memo, no training module that said
do not read the names.
But it was understood.
Technicians referred to souls by cell number.
B1-4427. B2-0093.
Coordinates on a grid. Positions on a rack.

To use a name was unprofessional, sentimental,
and — by an unspoken consensus that had the force of taboo —
something that made the work harder for everyone.

Maren had learned this in her first month
and had not thought about it since.

[Pause]

Jorin Dael dropped into the adjacent chair and set down his own mug.
Morning.
Morning.
Gren left a note about a flicker on B2, north bank.
Probably a calibration drift.
I'll have a look after break.

[Pause] [Low, hypnotic]

This was the shape of it.

The monitoring centre hummed
with the muted vibration of several hundred containment cells
two floors below,
transmitted through concrete and steel into the floor
and up through the soles of Maren's boots.

It was a low, continuous note —
not a sound you heard
so much as a pressure you felt in the back of your teeth,
in the soft tissue behind your eardrums.

New technicians sometimes complained about it.
By the second week they stopped noticing.
By the second year it was the silence they noticed,
on days off, in their own flats —
the absence of the hum felt louder than the hum itself.

[Pause] [Fade]
```

## Segment 3 of 3 — "Dreamless"

**Style of Music:**
```
Dark ambient spoken word, documentary underscore, sparse industrial drone, low piano notes, 
haunting atmospheric pads, female narrator, contemplative fading pace, melancholy dread, 
cinematic, 58 BPM
```

**Lyrics:**
```
[Narration] [Matter-of-fact]

Maren pulled up the overnight intake log.
Three new arrivals, processed during the small hours:
case numbers, cell assignments, projected yields.
She scanned the entries the way she scanned everything —
for anomalies, not for meaning.

[Pause]

The morning passed in increments of data.

Maren descended to B1 at ten o'clock
to recalibrate a flagged cell —
a routine adjustment,
five minutes with a calibration tool and a logged entry.

[Pause] [Slower, atmospheric]

The containment hall was cool and dry,
lit by flat fluorescent strips
that made the rows of glass-and-copper cylinders gleam.
Each cell was the size of a household water heater,
racked shoulder-high in steel frames,
etched with suppression sigils that looked,
if you didn't know better,
like decorative filigree.

The sigils were not decorative.
They were the mechanism by which a human soul was kept flowing outward
as raw current
rather than allowed to coalesce into anything
that might be called awareness.

[Pause]

She recalibrated the cell, checked the readout, and climbed back upstairs.
She filed the report.
She drank another cup of canal-water coffee.
She ate lunch in the break room with Jorin —
sandwiches from home,
a conversation about the tram schedule,
the mutual pretence that this was an ordinary workplace
where ordinary people did ordinary things.

[Pause]

At half past three, Maren ran the afternoon scan.
Green. Green. Amber — she flagged it. Green.
The screens pulsed their patient rhythm.
The hum came up through the floor.

[Pause] [Winding down, intimate]

Her shift ended at six.
She logged out, pulled on her jacket,
and walked to the tram stop.
The evening tram rattled west through the drizzle.
Near the door, the brass plaque caught the overhead light for a moment,
and Maren did not read it,
the same way she had not read it four thousand times before.

[Pause]

She went home.

The flat was quiet
in the way that flats are quiet
when one person has lived alone in them long enough
that the silence has become furniture.

She heated soup. She sat on the sofa.
She did not think about the cells, the readouts,
or the names she did not read.
She had trained herself out of that years ago,
the way a surgeon trains herself out of seeing the patient
as a person on the table —
not out of cruelty,
but out of the need to keep cutting.

[Pause] [Whispered, final]

The radiator clanked.
The street lamp outside her window
threw its steady orange light against the ceiling.

Somewhere beneath the city, in rows of glass and copper,
several hundred souls hummed their slow, inaudible erosion
into the grid that kept the lights on,
and Maren Voss slept the way she always slept:
deeply, dreamlessly, and without difficulty.

[Pause]

It was the last night she would sleep that way.

[Silence] [End]
```

## Production Notes

- **Total segments:** 3 (each ~2.5–3.5 minutes at narration pace)
- **Voice:** Female narrator — aim for measured, detached, documentary tone. Not dramatic; the horror is in the normalcy.
- **Music bed:** Keep instrumentation minimal and subordinate to the narration. The industrial drone/hum motif should echo the containment cells' hum described in the text. Let it swell subtly during the sigil description (Segment 3) and recede to near-silence for the final lines.
- **Segment boundaries:** Each segment ends at a natural pause point. If Suno renders a segment shorter than expected, the breaks still work dramatically.
- **Dialogue:** Jorin and Maren's exchange (Segment 2) is kept flat and casual — no dramatic inflection. These are people who say "morning" the way you say it to a coworker you've said it to a thousand times.
- **The last line** ("It was the last night she would sleep that way") should land in near-silence — the music bed should have faded almost entirely by this point.
