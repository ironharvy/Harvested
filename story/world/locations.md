# World Bible — Locations

## Ashward

**Type:** Industrial city, the story's primary setting.

**Physical description and climate:** A sprawling rust-belt metropolis built along the River Tyne — not the English one, but a sluggish, tea-brown waterway that bends through the city's industrial district before emptying into a silted estuary thirty kilometres south. The skyline is dominated by cooling towers, conduit spires, and the weathered concrete blocks of mid-century civic architecture. The streets in the older quarters are cobbled, slick with rain more often than not. Newer districts sprawl outward in grids of pebble-dash terraces and flat-roofed commercial buildings. The sky is perpetually overcast — a low ceiling of grey that breaks, perhaps, ten days a year. Summers are cool and damp; winters are long, dark, and raw. The air smells of ozone and something faintly mineral, a byproduct of the harvest stations' transduction process that residents no longer notice.

**Who lives/works there:** Civil servants, factory workers, harvest station personnel, shopkeepers, school teachers — the ordinary population of an industrial city that peaked two generations ago and has been quietly contracting since. Wealthier residents and Bureau administrators tend toward the west-side neighbourhoods above the river. The eastern districts, where the older harvest stations are clustered, are working-class and increasingly depopulated.

**Geographic relationships:** Ashward sits inland, a day's train from the capital (where the Bureau's Central Office operates). It was once a hub of early harvest-era industry — one of the first cities to build a soul-powered grid — and its infrastructure reflects that history: old, extensive, and difficult to replace. Newer, more efficient cities have drawn investment and population away. Ashward endures the way old industrial towns do: stubbornly, with diminished expectations.

**Atmosphere:** Functional and resigned. Not a place people move to; a place people stay. There is a civic pride, but it is the brittle kind — defensive, rooted in history rather than present vitality. The hum of the grid is audible in quiet moments, a low thrumming that residents describe as "the city breathing." It is, in a sense, exactly that.

**Plot significance:** Ashward's decline mirrors Maren's own erosion. The city's ageing infrastructure is the reason Harvest Station Nine still operates at above-capacity extraction loads, which is the reason souls placed there degrade faster, which is the mechanism by which Irina Voss's soul is being consumed. The city is complicit at the civic scale the way Maren is complicit at the personal one.

## Harvest Station Nine

**Type:** Bureau of Reclamation harvest station (power generation facility).

**Physical description:** A rectangular concrete complex occupying a full city block on Ashward's eastern industrial fringe. Three storeys above ground, two below. The exterior is brutalist — poured concrete, narrow windows, a flat roof bristling with ventilation stacks and the copper-clad conduit spire that channels psychic current into the city grid. The concrete has weathered to a mottled grey-brown, streaked with mineral staining from decades of transduction exhaust. A chain-link perimeter fence topped with Bureau signage: **HARVEST STATION NINE — BUREAU OF RECLAMATION — AUTHORISED PERSONNEL ONLY.** The sign is faded.

**Interior layout:**
- **Ground floor:** Reception and intake. A linoleum-floored lobby with strip lighting, plastic chairs, and a reception window where intake clerks process forms. The intake bay — a sterile room with a transfer cradle where souls are moved from portable flasks to permanent containment cells — is behind a heavy security door. Poul Hadren works here.
- **First floor:** Administrative offices. Director Leth's office is here — a room with a window overlooking the station's interior courtyard, a metal desk, filing cabinets, and the faint smell of weak tea. A small staff kitchen, a records room with wall-to-wall shelving of courier data-sticks.
- **Second floor:** Monitoring centre. A long room lined with readout consoles, each linked to a bank of containment cells below. This is where conduit technicians spend most of their shifts, watching gauges, logging data, and occasionally descending to the containment floors for recalibration. Strip lighting, ergonomic chairs that stopped being ergonomic years ago, and the constant low hum of the cells transmitted through the floor.
- **Basement Level 1 (B1):** Primary containment hall. Rows upon rows of containment cells — glass-and-copper cylinders racked floor to ceiling in steel frames, each with its own readout panel and suppression sigil array. The hall is wide, low-ceilinged, and lit by cool fluorescent strips. The air is dry and faintly charged, the way air feels before a storm. The hum here is not transmitted; it is the source. Several hundred souls are housed on this level. Irina Voss's cell — B1-4427 — is in the eastern quadrant.
- **Basement Level 2 (B2):** Surge infrastructure and overflow containment. Older cells, some decommissioned, some running at higher extraction loads to handle grid demand spikes. B2 is where souls are routed when the station is above capacity. The lighting is poorer, the maintenance less frequent, and the hum has an uneven quality — rhythmic interruptions, like breathing with a catch in it. Technicians dislike working B2 shifts but do not say why in specific terms.

**Climate (interior):** Temperature-controlled but imperfectly — the lower levels are perpetually cool, almost cold, regardless of season. Station workers wear layers. Whether the cold is a function of the ventilation system or of something else is not a question anyone asks aloud.

**Who works there:** Director Cassan Leth (station director), Maren Voss (conduit technician, day shift), Jorin Dael (conduit technician, day shift), Supervisor Gren (night-shift supervisor), Poul Hadren (intake clerk), and approximately fifteen other staff across shifts — technicians, clerks, a maintenance worker, a security guard at the gate. The station is understaffed for its capacity, a chronic condition the Bureau acknowledges in memos and ignores in budgets.

**Geographic relationships:** Located in Ashward's eastern industrial district, a twenty-minute tram ride from the city centre. Surrounded by shuttered factories, a disused rail siding, and a row of workers' terraces. The nearest active harvest station (Station Twelve) is across the city on the western edge; the two stations together supply most of Ashward's grid.

**Atmosphere:** The fluorescent sterility of a facility that was modern forty years ago. Everything works, but nothing works well. The containment cells hum. The readout screens flicker at their edges. Paperwork accrues in trays. The break room coffee is bad. The station smells of ozone, copper, and the particular staleness of recirculated air. There is a rhythm to it — intake, monitoring, calibration, filing — that makes it possible to forget what the cells contain. This is by design, or by habit, which in bureaucracies amounts to the same thing.

**Plot significance:** The story's primary arena. Maren's entire working life is enclosed here. Her mother's soul is held here. Her extraction attempt takes place here. The station is both workplace and prison — not for Maren in a literal sense, but in the way that twelve years of routine and complicity become a confinement of their own.

## Maren's Flat

**Type:** Residential, Maren's home.

**Physical description:** A one-bedroom flat on the third floor of a four-storey workers' terrace in Ashward's eastern district, a ten-minute walk from Harvest Station Nine. The building is brick, soot-darkened, with iron railings on narrow balconies that no one uses. The flat itself is small and clean in the way that empty spaces are clean — not scrubbed, just uncluttered. A narrow kitchen with a gas hob, a sitting room with a sofa and a shelf of books she finished years ago, a bedroom with a single window facing the street. Bureau-issued coveralls draped over the back of a chair. The radiator clanks. The light from the street lamp outside is orange and steady.

**Who lives there:** Maren, alone. She has lived here since her second year at the station. The flat has not changed much in a decade.

**Atmosphere:** Functional silence. The flat is not depressing so much as suspended — a space that has been waiting for something to happen in it. It reflects Maren's interior life: orderly, austere, and carefully emptied of anything that might require feeling.

**Plot significance:** The flat is the space between shifts, the place where Maren's isolation becomes visible. Key domestic scenes — the news of Irina's death, the late-night planning, the morning she does not go to work — happen here. The flat's proximity to the station is a small geographic fact that carries metaphorical weight: Maren lives in the shadow of her complicity.

## Irina's House

**Type:** Residential, Maren's childhood home.

**Physical description:** A ground-floor flat in a terraced row in Ashward's north quarter — a neighbourhood of small shops, a tram stop, and streets where the cobbles show through cracked tarmac. The flat is modest: two bedrooms, a kitchen with a window overlooking a narrow back yard, a sitting room where Irina kept the curtains half-drawn. The walls are papered in a pattern that was already dated when Maren was a child. A kitchen table with two chairs, though only one was used. The damp patch on the kitchen wall that Irina always meant to have seen to.

**Who lives/lived there:** Irina Voss lived here alone after Maren moved out. The flat is now empty — Maren has not yet cleared it.

**Atmosphere:** Quiet in the way a house is quiet after its occupant has died — not peaceful but vacated. The objects are still arranged by Irina's habits: the kettle angled just so, the shoes by the door, the coat on the hook. The air smells of dust and, faintly, of the lavender soap Irina used.

**Plot significance:** Maren visits or remembers this place as she reckons with her mother's death and the deferral petition that was never processed. The house is the physical residue of the relationship — the things Irina touched, the silence Maren let grow between them. It grounds the story's emotional stakes in the domestic and particular.

## The Intake Bay

**Type:** Functional space within Harvest Station Nine (ground floor).

**Physical description:** A sterile, windowless room behind the ground-floor security door. White-tiled walls, a drain in the floor (a vestige of earlier, messier transfer procedures), and a central transfer cradle — a steel-and-copper apparatus where portable flasks are locked in and their contents channelled into the station's permanent cell network. The lighting is harsh. Intake paperwork is completed at a metal desk near the door before the transfer begins. There is a viewing window — thick glass, observation-room style — that allows a clerk or supervisor to monitor the procedure, though it is rarely used.

**Who works there:** Intake clerks (including Poul Hadren) and the receiving technician on shift. Transfers typically happen in batches, two or three per day.

**Atmosphere:** Clinical. The room is designed for efficiency, not reflection. Souls arrive as case numbers on forms, and they leave as cell assignments. The transfer procedure takes roughly ten minutes per soul. There is no ceremony.

**Plot significance:** This is where Irina's soul entered the station — where the deferral petition should have held, and didn't. Maren may never enter this room during the story's present action, but it exists in her awareness as the threshold her mother crossed: the point where a person became a resource.

## B1 Containment Hall — Eastern Quadrant

**Type:** Subsection of Harvest Station Nine's primary containment floor.

**Physical description:** The eastern quadrant of B1 houses cells numbered 4400 through 4599 — two hundred containment units racked in eight rows of twenty-five, floor to ceiling. The racks are steel, bolted to the concrete floor, each cell slotted into its cradle with copper contact leads running to the transduction conduit beneath the floor. The aisle between racks is narrow — wide enough for one person and a calibration cart. The fluorescent lights cast a flat, shadowless light that makes the glass cylinders gleam. Irina's cell, B1-4427, is in the second row, third from the left, at roughly shoulder height.

**Atmosphere:** The hum here is steady and undifferentiated — the combined output of two hundred contained souls producing a sound that is felt in the teeth as much as heard. The air is cool and dry. The readout panels blink green for nominal, amber for degraded, red for critical. Most are green. A few are amber. The technicians check the amber ones and note the readings. They do not listen to the hum as individual voices because the suppression makes that impossible, and because not listening is what makes the job bearable.

**Plot significance:** This is where the climax takes place. Maren locates cell B1-4427, miscalibrates the suppression field, accesses the manual override panel, and releases her mother's soul. The geography of the space — the narrow aisles, the height of the cell, the proximity of other souls in their own cells — shapes the physical reality of the extraction scene. She is freeing one soul in a room full of hundreds, and she knows it.

## The Monitoring Centre

**Type:** Functional space within Harvest Station Nine (second floor).

**Physical description:** A long, open-plan room running the length of the station's upper floor. Two rows of workstations face banks of readout screens, each screen linked to a section of the containment floors below. The screens display output levels, suppression integrity, and estimated yield-to-depletion for each cell in the assigned bank. The furniture is civil-service standard: laminate desks, swivel chairs with worn upholstery, a noticeboard pinned with shift schedules and Bureau memos. A window at the far end overlooks the internal courtyard and the conduit spire.

**Who works there:** Conduit technicians on shift — typically three or four during the day, two at night. Maren and Jorin sit at adjacent stations.

**Atmosphere:** The hum is muted here — transmitted through the floor rather than directly present — but never absent. The room has the low-energy focus of a control room where nothing dramatic is supposed to happen. Technicians watch screens, mark anomalies, drink bad coffee, and exchange the minimal conversation of people sharing a vigil.

**Plot significance:** Maren's daily workplace and the place where she first notices her mother's soul in the system — an intake record that shouldn't be there yet, a cell assignment that skipped the deferral queue. The monitoring centre is where Maren's routine life cracks open. It is also the place she must behave normally while planning the extraction — watching the same screens, filing the same reports, while carrying the weight of what she intends to do.

## The Break Room

**Type:** Staff common area within Harvest Station Nine (ground floor).

**Physical description:** A small room off the main corridor — a counter with an electric kettle, a coffee machine that produces something technically qualifying as coffee, a microwave, a refrigerator with a handwritten sign about labelling food. A Formica table with six mismatched chairs. A window too high to see out of. A Bureau-issued safety poster on the wall, its corners curling.

**Who uses it:** All station staff, across shifts. The informal social centre of the station.

**Atmosphere:** The only room in the station that tries, faintly, to be human. Workers eat lunch here, complain about schedules, avoid talking about the work. The conversation is the ordinary patter of any workplace: weather, weekend plans, the quality of the coffee. It is in these moments that the normalisation of the harvest is most visible — not in propaganda or policy, but in the fact that people eat sandwiches above rooms full of captive souls and talk about the weather.

**Plot significance:** Scene-setting and character work. Maren and Jorin's quiet conversations happen here. The break room is where Maren gauges whether to trust Jorin, and where the careful ordinariness of station life is most sharply felt.

## The Capital

**Type:** City (off-stage), seat of government and Bureau Central Office.

**Physical description:** Not directly seen in the story. Referenced as a distant, more prosperous city — larger than Ashward, with newer infrastructure, a Parliament, and the Bureau's policy-making apparatus. Ashward residents speak of the capital the way residents of declining industrial towns speak of the national capital: with a mixture of resentment and irrelevance.

**Geographic relationships:** A day's train journey from Ashward. The capital's harvest stations are newer and more efficient — higher yield per soul, lower extraction loads, better-maintained equipment. The disparity is a source of quiet bitterness among Ashward's Bureau staff.

**Plot significance:** The capital represents the system's centre of gravity — the place where policy is made by people who do not work in stations like Nine. It exists in the story as an absence: the decisions that shaped Maren's world were made there, by people who will never see the inside of B1's eastern quadrant.

## The Tram

**Type:** Public transit route.

**Physical description:** Ashward's tram network is old, reliable, and uncrowded at off-peak hours. The eastern line runs from the industrial district past Harvest Station Nine's perimeter fence to the city centre, stopping at seven points along the way. The trams themselves are boxy, rattle-windowed, and lit with the same fluorescent strips as the station. They run on the grid — which is to say, on soul-power — a fact noted on a small plaque near the door that no one reads.

**Atmosphere:** Liminal. The tram is where Maren transitions between her working self and her private self, though the distinction has grown thin. The ride is twenty minutes of watching Ashward's streets pass in the grey light, the hum of the tram's electric motor indistinguishable from the hum she left behind.

**Plot significance:** A transitional space that appears in commute scenes and reinforces the story's geographic intimacy — Maren's world is small, contained, and inescapable. The tram's soul-powered plaque is a quiet reminder that the infrastructure she maintains is everywhere, not just behind the station's fence.
