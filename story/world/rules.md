# World Bible — Rules of the World

## Soul Mechanics

### Siphoning

At the moment of biological death, a Bureau of Reclamation field team performs the "draw" — a procedure requiring physical proximity (within three metres) and a portable siphon array. The array locks onto the psychic signature as it detaches from the body and channels it into a temporary containment flask. The window is narrow: roughly four minutes after cardiac arrest. After that, the soul disperses naturally and is lost. Not every death is attended — rural areas, accidents, sudden collapses — and roughly 15–20% of souls go unharvested. The Bureau treats this as acceptable loss, the way a utility tolerates transmission leakage.

The draw is painless in the Bureau's official literature. No one has asked the dead.

### Containment

Harvested souls are transported in sealed flasks to the nearest harvest station and transferred into permanent containment cells — cylindrical glass-and-copper units roughly the size of a household water heater, racked in rows of hundreds. Each cell is etched with suppression sigils derived from the original Consolidation-era research: patterns that keep the soul's psychic output flowing outward rather than allowing it to coalesce into awareness.

A properly suppressed soul produces a steady, low-level psychic current. An unsuppressed soul may flicker, moan, or — in rare and deeply unsettling cases — speak. Suppression is therefore framed as both an engineering necessity and a mercy.

### Erosion

Psychic output is not infinite. Each soul contains a finite reservoir of energy, and the extraction process degrades it. A healthy adult soul, properly contained, lasts thirty to fifty years before it erodes entirely — personality fraying into noise, then static, then nothing. The Bureau's internal metric is "yield-to-depletion," measured in kilowatt-equivalents. Children's souls yield less. The elderly yield less. These are actuarial facts, printed in tables.

Erosion is not uniform. Souls under higher extraction loads — stations running above capacity, emergency grid surges — degrade faster. A soul routed into a surge node might last five years instead of forty.

### Rapid Erosion and Fast-Tracking

Certain souls are flagged for accelerated processing: skipping the standard sixty-day grieving deferral, assigned to high-draw cells, or routed into stations already running above capacity. The criteria for fast-tracking are nominally bureaucratic — no next-of-kin on file, unclaimed remains, administrative expediency — but the process is opaque enough to allow quiet favouritism and political convenience. A director can fast-track a soul with nothing more than a stamped form and a cited regulation.

## The Bureau of Reclamation

### Structure

The Bureau is a government ministry with the organisational culture of a public utility — methodical, underfunded, and deeply convinced of its own necessity. It operates at three tiers:

- **Central Office** (in the capital, not Ashward): sets policy, manages public messaging, negotiates with Parliament over harvest quotas.
- **Regional Directorate:** oversees clusters of harvest stations, allocates souls across the grid, handles inter-station transfers.
- **Station level:** the harvest stations themselves, each run by a Director with a small staff of conduit technicians, intake clerks, and maintenance workers.

### Regulatory Framework

The legal basis for soul-harvesting is the **Civic Contribution Act**, passed 187 years ago during the early industrial expansion, when the ghost-light phenomenon was first harnessed at scale. The Act designates the psychic residue of the deceased as a "public resource" — legally equivalent to mineral rights or water access. Families may petition for a sixty-day grieving deferral before siphoning, but they cannot refuse the harvest itself. Refusal is not criminalised; it is simply not a legal concept. The dead belong to the grid.

There is no opt-out. There is no religious exemption. A small abolitionist movement exists but is marginal — seen the way climate activists are seen in a petrostate: sincere, impractical, and vaguely embarrassing.

### Personnel

Harvest station workers are civil servants. The pay is modest, the benefits adequate, the work steady. Most positions require a technical certification (the Conduit Technician's Licence, a six-month programme). Workers develop the same coping mechanisms as mortuary attendants or slaughterhouse employees: dark humour, emotional compartmentalisation, and an unspoken agreement not to discuss the work outside the station.

Turnover is low. People who can't stomach it leave in the first year. Those who stay tend to stay for decades.

## Technology

### The Grid

Ashward's grid is functionally identical to a modern electrical grid — substations, transformers, transmission lines — except that the generation source is psychic rather than chemical or nuclear. Harvest stations serve the role of power plants. Turbines convert psychic current to electrical current through a process the Bureau calls "transduction," involving rotating arrays of sigil-etched copper coils. The physics is empirical, not fully understood; the engineering works, and that has been sufficient.

The grid is old. Much of Ashward's infrastructure dates to the first expansion era, and stations like Harvest Station Nine are ageing, patched, and chronically under-maintained. Newer stations in wealthier cities are cleaner, more efficient, and — crucially — extract more energy per soul, which means they need fewer of them.

### Monitoring

Each containment cell has a readout panel displaying the soul's current output, estimated remaining yield, and suppression integrity. Conduit technicians monitor these the way power plant operators monitor gauges: watching for anomalies, filing reports, performing routine maintenance. A degraded suppression field shows as a flickering readout and may produce audible phenomena — a hum, a whisper, a sound like breathing. Technicians are trained to recalibrate; they are not trained to listen.

### Communication and Record-Keeping

The Bureau maintains a centralised intake registry: every harvested soul is logged with a case number, date of death, date of siphoning, assigned station, cell number, and projected yield-to-depletion. Access to the registry is tiered — station-level workers can see their own station's records but not the broader network. Directors have regional access. The system is digital but not networked in a modern sense; records transfer via secure courier data-sticks, not live databases. This deliberate inefficiency is presented as a security measure. It also makes it very difficult to track a specific soul across the system without institutional access.

## Social Norms and Etiquette

### The Consensus

The soul harvest is not a secret. It is a fact of civic life, discussed the way taxation or military service is discussed: with resignation, occasional grumbling, and a bedrock assumption that it is necessary. School curricula include a unit on "civic energy" in secondary school. Public messaging emphasises duty, gratitude, and the idea that the dead "continue to serve." Memorial culture has adapted: families speak of their dead as "still giving," and there is a civic holiday — **Contribution Day** — honouring the harvested.

People do not, as a rule, think about what it means. The few who do tend to stop talking about it.

### The Grieving Deferral

Families are entitled to a sixty-day deferral between death and siphoning — time to hold a funeral, settle affairs, and say goodbye. The deferral is the one concession to sentiment in the system, and it is jealously guarded by the public. Skipping it — fast-tracking a soul without family notification — is technically legal but socially transgressive, the kind of bureaucratic shortcut that would cause outrage if it became public. It rarely becomes public.

### Taboos

- **Naming the dead in a station.** Technicians refer to souls by cell number, never by name. Using a name is considered unprofessional, sentimental, and — by some — psychologically dangerous. It is the workplace equivalent of naming an animal you intend to slaughter.
- **Discussing suppression failures.** When a soul speaks or manifests, it is logged as a "field anomaly" and recalibrated. Workers do not discuss what the soul said. There is no formal prohibition; the silence is cultural.
- **Visiting a relative's cell.** Bureau policy technically permits family members to request the cell number of a harvested relative, but the process is buried in paperwork and no station encourages it. The unspoken rule: once they're in the grid, let go.

## Loopholes and Vulnerabilities

### The Manual Override

Containment cells have a manual release — a physical lever behind a locked panel, a legacy of early safety design when engineers worried about cascade failures. The override is never used in normal operations and most technicians have never touched one. But it exists, and a technician with the right access key can open the panel.

Releasing a soul from containment does not destroy it. The soul disperses into the environment — rapidly, chaotically, and without the slow erosion of grid extraction. A dispersed soul does not persist; it scatters into ambient psychic noise within minutes. But for those minutes, it is free. If the suppression sigils are removed before the release, the soul may briefly coalesce — aware, present, itself — before it goes.

### Record Gaps

The courier-based record system means there is always a lag between a soul's intake and its registration in the central database. A soul moved between stations during this window might temporarily exist in neither station's official log. This is a known inefficiency; the Bureau considers it a minor bookkeeping issue. It is also, for anyone trying to hide what they've done, a window.

### The Deferral Paperwork

The sixty-day grieving deferral is initiated by family petition, but the petition must be filed within forty-eight hours of death. If no petition is filed — because the family didn't know, because there is no family, because a clerk didn't process it in time — the deferral expires by default and the soul enters the grid immediately. The system's design assumes families will advocate for themselves. When they don't, the system does what systems do.

### Suppression Calibration

Suppression fields must be maintained. A deliberately miscalibrated field — output tuned down, suppression weakened — would allow a soul to regain partial coherence while still appearing, on the readout, to be within normal parameters. The variance would show on a close inspection, but close inspections happen on a quarterly cycle. A technician who knew the tolerances could keep a soul semi-aware for weeks without triggering an automatic flag.
